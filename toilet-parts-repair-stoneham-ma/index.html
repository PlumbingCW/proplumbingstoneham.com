Toilet Parts Repair in Stoneham, MA: Flapper and Fill Valve

The parts inside the tank and under the bowl, what each one does, and how to match the symptom to the fix.

Call (339) 216-9771

By the Stoneham Pro Plumbing team · Published November 14, 2026 · Updated November 14, 2026

Most toilet repairs come down to five parts. Knowing what each does turns a vague complaint, such as the toilet runs on, into a specific, inexpensive fix. This guide walks through the tank, the bowl and the seal at the floor, with the symptom that points to each part.

Inside the tank: what each part does

A tank is a simple machine, and the flapper and fill valve account for most of the trouble.

The food coloring test for a leaking flapper

Drop a few drops of food coloring into the tank and wait 15 minutes without flushing. If color shows in the bowl, water is leaking past the flapper. Replace it with the same style. Flappers are cheap, and the new one should seat firmly, with a little slack in the chain.

If the flapper looks fine but the toilet still runs, check whether water is rising to the top of the overflow tube. If it is, the fill valve is not shutting off and needs replacing.

Replacing a fill valve in four steps

Most fill valve kits include a diagram. If the tank bolts or the shutoff valve are corroded, stop and call a plumber, since forcing them can crack the porcelain.

  1. Turn off the shutoff valve behind the toilet and flush to empty the tank.
  2. Disconnect the supply line at the bottom of the tank and unscrew the lock nut under it.
  3. Lift out the old valve and set the new one to the same height as the old, using the manufacturer's height marks.
  4. Reconnect the line, turn the water on slowly and adjust the float so the water stops about an inch below the overflow tube.

The wax ring and a rocking toilet

The wax ring seals the base of the toilet to the drain flange. If water appears around the base, or the toilet rocks, the seal is suspect. Resetting a toilet means lifting it, scraping off the old ring, checking the flange, and setting the toilet on a new ring.

A cracked or low flange needs a repair first. A toilet that has leaked for a long time may have damaged the floor under it, so press the floor near the base. If it feels soft, have it looked at before the toilet goes back.

When to call a plumber

For recurring clogs that affect other drains, see drain cleaning. A toilet that is old and worn out may be cheaper to replace than to repair again.

  • The shutoff valve will not close or leaks when turned.
  • The bowl or tank is cracked.
  • Water appears on the ceiling below the bathroom.
  • More than one fixture is slow or gurgling, which suggests the main drain.
  • You have replaced parts and the toilet still runs or clogs.

Choosing replacement parts that fit

Toilet parts look universal and are not. Flappers come in two-inch and three-inch sizes, and a three-inch drain needs the larger flapper. Fill valves differ in height, and a valve that is too tall will not fit under the tank lid. Tank bolts, gaskets and supply lines vary with the model.

The simplest way to avoid a second trip to the store is to photograph the inside of the tank and the label on the back, and to bring the old part with you.

Many toilets list the model on the inside of the tank lid or behind the seat. If the toilet is a decade or more old and parts are hard to match, a plumber can tell you whether a universal kit will do or whether replacing the toilet is the cleaner answer.

Toilet repair and Stoneham's water

Stoneham's water is supplied through the MWRA and is described as soft, at about 1 grain per gallon. Soft water leaves little mineral buildup on fill valves and flappers, so parts in Stoneham tanks tend to wear from age and chlorine exposure, and less often from scale. That is why the rubber flapper is the part most likely to fail first.

If you are not sure about your own water, ask Stoneham Public Works, which runs the town's water system. Homes in nearby Woburn and Wakefield should check with their own water departments.
