Nobility Hill sits roughly between Chestnut Street, Maple Street and Cedar Avenue, a few blocks west of Main Street and Stoneham Center. It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places as the Nobility Hill Historic District on February 9, 1990, and its houses were built between about 1860 and 1920.

This page is for homeowners on those streets and the blocks around them. The edges of a neighborhood are rarely marked, so if you are not sure your street counts, call and describe where you are. We will tell you whether we can help.

What the houses on Nobility Hill look like

The district's National Register nomination describes 33 contributing buildings in styles that include Italianate, Second Empire, Queen Anne, Stick Style, Shingle, Colonial Revival, Craftsman and Dutch Colonial. It calls the area a small, modest residential enclave. Its first residents included shoe manufacturers, a tannery owner, bank officers and tradespeople, and later ranch houses and a church were added in the twentieth century.

Houses from this period were built long before modern plumbing codes, and most have been updated at least once since then. What was changed, and when, differs from house to house. Treat the age as a reason to look, not as proof that something is wrong.

Questions worth asking about an older house

A plumber can answer most of these in one visit to the basement:

  • What are the supply and drain pipes made of, and where does the material change?
  • Does the main shutoff close all the way, and does everyone in the house know where it is?
  • Has the line from the house to the street ever been looked at? A sewer camera inspection answers that.
  • Are slow drains a one-fixture problem or a sign for the whole house? Drain cleaning with a camera tells you which.
  • Is there a damp spot, a high water bill or a sound of running water at night? Leak detection finds hidden leaks with the least cutting.

If the supply piping turns out to be near the end of its life, repiping is planned work that you can schedule and price ahead of time, instead of waiting for a burst.

Working in a historic house

National Register listing, by itself, does not limit what a private owner does inside the house, and it does not require particular plumbing methods. If a project touches the outside of the building, ask the Stoneham Building Division what approvals apply before work starts.

Plumbing in Massachusetts is regulated under 248 CMR, and most work needs a permit. In houses with plaster walls, ask the plumber whether the pipes can be reached from the basement or an existing access before anyone opens a wall, and ask how a necessary opening will be patched.

Before you call

  1. Shut off water at the fixture or the main if water is running, and take a photo of what you see.
  2. Note which fixtures are affected and when the problem started.
  3. Write down the street and the nearest cross street.
  4. Call and describe it. Ask about timing and what the visit will include.
