Read Your Water Meter and Find a Hidden Leak in Stoneham, MA

Your water meter can tell you if you have a hidden leak. A 15-minute test, no tools needed.

Call (339) 216-9771

By the Stoneham Pro Plumbing team · Published November 14, 2026 · Updated November 14, 2026

A hidden leak can run for weeks before it shows up as a stain or a bill. The water meter is the cheapest leak detector you own, because it measures every drop that enters the house. If it moves when nothing is on, water is going somewhere.

This guide shows how to find and read your meter, run a simple test and work out what the result means. If the test points to a leak, our leak detection page explains how a plumber locates it.

Finding and reading the meter

The meter is usually in the basement near where the water line enters, or in a pit outside near the street. It has a face with a row of numbers like an odometer, a sweep hand and a small leak indicator, often a triangle or a star, that spins with any flow.

Some meters have a radio transmitter, and the display may be a small digital screen. The numbers record usage in gallons or cubic feet, and the label tells you which unit your meter uses.

Part of the meterWhat it shows
Odometer numbersTotal water used since installed
Sweep handFlow in the moment, one turn equals a set volume
Leak indicatorSpins or flashes with even tiny flow
Unit labelGallons or cubic feet
Serial numberIdentifies the meter to the utility

The 15-minute leak test

If the reading has not changed, you have no significant leak at that moment. If the number moved, water is escaping.

  1. Turn off every faucet and appliance that uses water, including the ice maker.
  2. Check that no toilet is running, and that the irrigation system is idle.
  3. Look at the leak indicator. If it is moving, you already have your answer.
  4. Write down the odometer reading, or take a photo.
  5. Wait 15 minutes without using any water, and read it again.

Narrowing down where the leak is

Close the main shutoff valve in the house and look at the meter again. If it still moves, the leak is between the meter and the shutoff, which is the service line. If it stops, the leak is inside the house. Then close individual shutoffs one at a time, at the toilets and the water heater, to see which one stops the meter.

A running toilet is the most common cause. Try the food coloring test in the tank. If the meter moves slowly, a toilet flapper is the likely culprit. See our guide on toilet repair for parts.

Slab, wall and underground leaks

A leak that survives all the individual shutoffs and does not show on the surface is in a wall, a slab or underground. Signs include warm spots on a floor, the sound of running water and a damp patch in the yard. These need listening equipment and thermal imaging.

If the service line is the problem, water line repair explains how it is found and fixed. Do not dig yourself, because gas and electric lines may be nearby.

When the meter is not the answer

A rise in a bill is not always a leak. Seasonal use, guests, irrigation and billing estimates all matter. If the 15-minute test shows no movement and the bill is still high, ask your water utility to read the meter and check its accuracy.

A meter that is damaged or very old can read wrong. Public utilities replace meters on a cycle, so a utility visit is the right step if the meter itself looks suspect.

Keeping a record of your readings

Once you know how to read your meter, write down the reading at the same time each month. A simple log shows how much water your household uses in a normal month, so a jump stands out right away instead of arriving as a surprise on the bill.

Also note your reading before and after a trip. If you go away for a week with every fixture off and the meter moves, you have caught a leak with no guesswork. And if the number rises in a pattern, for example only overnight, the pattern points to the source, such as a toilet that refills in the small hours or an irrigation timer that fires at night. Small habits like this save real money.

Stoneham Public Works and your meter

Stoneham Public Works says it replaces water meters on a 15-year cycle and reads them quarterly and monthly. If your meter is older than that, or you see damage, a call to Public Works is the right first step. Public Works can confirm the reading and check the meter's accuracy.

If the meter is fine and still moving with everything off, call a plumber. Homeowners in nearby Wakefield and Reading should ask their own water departments how meters are read and replaced.
